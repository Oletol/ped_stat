Build the analysis from the research question—not from the p-value.

This decision tree checks the research aim, scale, study design, distributional assumptions and approximate sample size. It then recommends methods that can be run completely in the browser.

Statistical decision tree

Step 1. What claim are you trying to support?

Why this matters: a correlation question, a pre–post question and an instrument-quality question require different statistics.

Step 2. What kind of data represent the outcome?

Why this matters: rank-based and categorical tests do not make the same assumptions as mean-based parametric tests.

Step 3. How were observations collected?

Why this matters: paired data must remain paired. Treating repeated observations as independent produces invalid standard errors.

Step 4. Are parametric assumptions defensible?

For a paired t-test, inspect the distribution of the paired differences rather than the two score distributions separately.

Step 5. Approximate sample size

The site flags very small samples. A non-significant result with low N should not be rewritten as “there is no effect”.

What can a statistical test establish?

Difference ≠ effectiveness.
A significant result can support a claim that scores changed or groups differed. To attribute that difference to the teaching method, the design must address competing explanations.

Stronger causal evidence usually requires

A comparison/control condition, baseline measurement, comparable groups or randomization where feasible, valid and reliable outcome measures, transparent handling of attrition and missing data, and an a priori analysis plan.

Minimum reporting set

N, descriptive statistics, test statistic, degrees of freedom where applicable, p-value, 95% confidence interval when estimable, effect size, assumptions/limitations, and a restrained interpretation.
